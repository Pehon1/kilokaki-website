How to Log Shared Dishes

Round table, ten dishes, one steamboat — and nobody knows how much they actually ate. Here's how to log zi char, hotpot and family-style meals without a scale or a fight.

A lazy Susan with eight dishes spinning on it. A steamboat in the middle of the table. Everyone reaching in, nobody measuring, plates that never empty. How on earth do you log that?

Shared eating is the boss level of food logging. It's not one plate that's yours — it's a moving pile that everyone is eating from at once. There's no portion to weigh because there's no portion at all.

And yet communal meals are how a huge amount of eating happens in Singapore: zi char dinners, steamboat with the family, reunion meals, dim sum, mookata with friends. If you skip logging every shared meal, you're skipping a big chunk of your week. Here's how to do it without the stress.

Why Shared Meals Feel Impossible

Three reasons the round table breaks your log:

1. No defined portion. You didn't order "a plate." You took some sweet and sour pork, some sambal kangkong, a bit of the fish, more rice, a second helping of the chilli crab. There's no single thing to look up.

2. You lose count. Across a two-hour dinner you go back to the dishes again and again. By the end you genuinely don't know if you had two scoops of rice or four.

3. Logging at the table feels rude. Pulling out your phone to tap in every item while auntie is serving you food reads as anti-social. So you don't — and the meal becomes a blank.

This is a cousin of the social dinner problem and the family food pressure problem — the food is emotional and communal, which makes precise tracking feel impossible and a little awkward.

The "Build Your Own Plate" Method

The trick is to stop thinking about the table and start thinking about your own plate. You don't log the whole steamboat. You log what passed through you.

Step 1: Count your rice (or your carb anchor). Rice is your unit of measure. Two bowls of rice across the meal is a clear, loggable number — and it tells you roughly how much everything else you ate, because the dishes scale with the rice.

Step 2: Tally your protein in palms. Across the dinner, how many palm-sized servings of meat or fish did you have? Two or three? That's your protein estimate. (One palm ≈ one serving — the same trick from eyeballing portions.)

Step 3: Name the heavy hitters. Was there something fried, creamy, or sauce-heavy you went back for? Salted egg prawns, cereal chicken, char siew, the gravy you spooned over your rice. Note those — they're where the calories concentrate.

Step 4: Log it as one rough entry. "Zi char dinner: 2 bowls rice, 3 palms of mixed meat/fish, sweet and sour pork, sambal veg, shared a plate of cereal prawns." One message to KiloKaki, voice or text. Done.

You're not logging the table. You're logging your plate — counted in bowls of rice and palms of protein.

Steamboat & Mookata: A Special Case

Hotpot looks healthy — it's just boiling water and vegetables, right? It can be one of the heaviest meals you eat. The water is fine. The damage is in three places:

  • The dipping sauces. Sesame sauce, satay sauce, and chilli oil are calorie-dense. A few heaped spoons across the meal can add 200–300 calories on their own.
  • The processed add-ons. Fishballs, luncheon meat, fried beancurd skin, instant noodles, dumplings — these are where steamboat calories hide. Fatty marbled beef adds up fast too.
  • The "endless" pace. Buffet steamboat removes the natural stop signal. You keep cooking and eating because there's always more in the pot.

To log it: count your sauce bowls (1 bowl of mixed sauce ≈ 150–250 kcal), tally the processed items (each fishball/slice ≈ 30–60 kcal, a slab of luncheon meat ≈ 80 kcal), add your carbs (noodles, dumplings), and estimate your meat in palms. Vegetables you can log loosely — they barely move the number.

How to Log Without Being "That Person"

You don't need to log at the table. Use the same move that works for hawker food and a night of drinks: capture now, estimate later.

  • Snap one photo of the spread when the food arrives. One picture, two seconds, looks like everyone else taking food photos. It's your memory anchor.
  • Stay present during the meal. Don't tap your phone between bites. Just keep a loose mental tally: bowls of rice, palms of protein.
  • Backfill afterwards. In the car or back home, send one voice note describing your plate. The photo plus the description is more than enough.

Snap the spread, enjoy the dinner, log your plate after. Nobody needs to know.

The Mindset That Makes It Work

Shared meals are where most people throw up their hands and log nothing — and that "nothing" is exactly why their week doesn't add up. A rough estimate of a zi char dinner is infinitely more useful than a blank, because the blank pretends the meal never happened.

You will be off on any single shared meal. That's fine. Calorie counts are wrong anyway — even nutrition labels are ±20%. Over a week, your rough estimates average out into a pattern you can actually use. That pattern is the point.

So next steamboat, next reunion dinner, next dim sum Sunday: count your bowls of rice, count your palms of protein, note the heavy hitters, and log it after. Close enough beats perfect-but-skipped — every time.

Why You're Not Losing Weight Even Though You're Logging Everything

The Logging Gap Nobody Talks About

KiloKaki · July 8, 2026

You're not lying. You are logging. Every meal, every snack, every drink. But the scale has been exactly the same for three weeks and you're starting to wonder if food logging is just... pointless.

It's not pointless. But there is something happening that you're probably not seeing — and it has nothing to do with your willpower.

The Logging Gap: Adherence vs Accuracy

Most people food log to lose weight. They open the app, search for their meal, pick a portion, and log it. The act of logging feels productive. The calorie total seems reasonable.

Here's the problem: logging a meal is not the same as capturing what you ate.

The gap between those two things is what nutrition researchers call "measurement error" — and for most people using calorie counting apps, it's significant. Not because you're lying, but because:

  • The food database doesn't have your actual hawker meal, so you pick the closest match and hope
  • The portion you log is what you think you ate, not what you actually ate
  • Drinks, sauces, and "extras" don't make it into the log because they're not "food"
  • The olive oil the cook used, the kaya on your toast, the prawn crackers that came with the dish — all invisible

The Portion Estimation Problem

Studies on self-reported food intake consistently show people underestimate calorie consumption by 20–50%. Not because they're dishonest — because humans are genuinely bad at visually estimating portion sizes without tools.

A "regular portion" of char kway teow from a hawker stall is probably 700–900 calories. The MyFitnessPal entry you picked might say 450. You logged it faithfully. The math still doesn't work.

Why Photo Logging Closes This Gap

When you photograph your food before you eat it — and after, if portions changed — you capture something the database can't: the actual visual evidence of what was on your plate.

You can't argue with the photo. You can't "pick the lower calorie option" in a database. The photo shows the oil. The photo shows the extra scoop of rice. The photo shows the Milo you had "just one" of but was actually two.

Photo logging doesn't require you to know the exact calorie count. It shows you the actual meal — and that's what most people are missing when they say "I'm logging but not losing."

The Three Most Common Logging Gaps

1. The Drink Gap

Milo. Teh. Coffee with evaporated milk. Fresh orange juice. These are 100–300 calories that most people don't log because they don't feel like "meals." Three drinks a day is an extra 500 calories — the equivalent of skipping a meal.

2. The Oil and Sauce Gap

Hawker food is cooked in oil. The mee siam broth has sugar. The dry ban mian has sauce that's more oil than you'd guess. Without seeing the photo, you have no idea how much oil was in your meal. With the photo, you can see the film on the surface of the broth and estimate accordingly.

3. The Portion Creep Gap

Portions at hawker stalls aren't standardized. The auntie gives you what she feels is normal. "Normal" might be 30% more than what a restaurant would call a portion. You log what looks right — but it isn't.

What To Actually Do

If you've been logging consistently for 3+ weeks with no scale movement, here's the test:

  1. Go back through your last 7 days of food photos
  2. Look for the drinks (Milo, teh, juice)
  3. Look at the oil on the surface of broth dishes
  4. Look at the extra rice that "wasn't much"
  5. Pick the three biggest daily sources of untracked calories — just three

Most people find that addressing those three sources creates a 300–500 calorie daily gap. That's 2,100–3,500 calories a week. That's a lb of fat loss without changing anything else.

The logging was never the problem. The logging was missing the gaps.

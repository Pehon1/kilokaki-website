How to Log a Buffet Without Counting 47 Dishes

Food Logging · July 2026

Sunday, 12:30pm. Hotel brunch. There's a sashimi station, a laksa station, a carving station, a chocolate fountain that you've decided is for the kids but keep glancing at. You're two plates in, a third is planned, and somewhere between the chilli crab and the rösti a quiet thought surfaces:

How am I supposed to log any of this?

And because the honest answer feels like "I can't," most people don't. The buffet becomes a blank spot in the log. Then the blank spot becomes permission — today's already unloggable — and dinner goes unrecorded too. One meal you couldn't measure turns into a whole day you didn't track.

Buffets aren't a logging problem. They're a unit problem. You've been trying to log dishes. At a buffet, the unit is the plate.

Why buffets kill food logs

Too many items. A normal meal is one entry. A buffet plate might hold nine things in sampler portions. Log them individually and you're doing data entry at the table like it's a stocktake. Nobody sustains that — and nobody should have to. It's the same overload that makes people quit tracking apps entirely, just compressed into one meal.

No menu, no portions, no labels. Half a scoop of laksa. Three pieces of sashimi. A spoonful of curry you mostly didn't finish. Databases have no entry for "a bit of everything." Guessing dish-by-dish from a database is slower and not meaningfully more accurate than estimating the plate as a whole.

The "eat your money's worth" mindset. You paid $88. The maths brain wants volume. That's fine — but it means buffet sessions run long and large, and the later plates are exactly the ones that never get logged.

The blackout effect. This is the real damage. Skipping the buffet doesn't just lose one meal of data — it teaches you that "complicated" means "don't log." Every zi char dinner, every wedding, every company D&D inherits that lesson. It's the "what the hell" effect wearing a nicer shirt.

What a buffet actually costs (ballpark, not judgment)

  • A loaded savoury plate: 400–700 kcal. Mixed mains, some carbs, sauce. A restrained salad-and-sashimi plate can come in under 300; a rösti-and-carving-station plate won't.
  • A dessert plate: 300–600 kcal. Small pieces add up fast — three "tiny" pastries and a scoop of ice cream is a full meal's worth.
  • Drinks: 0–400 kcal. Free-flow soft drinks, juice, or wine quietly rival a plate. (Beer and wine maths is its own topic — see how to log alcohol.)
  • A full session: 1,500–2,500+ kcal. Three savoury plates, dessert, and drinks lands there without any heroics.

Is that a lot? It's a buffet. Of course it's a lot. The point of logging it isn't to shame the number — it's that a 2,000-calorie brunch you can see is easy to balance across a normal week, while an invisible one just becomes a confusing scale reading next Friday.

The plate-by-plate system

  1. Photo every plate before you eat it. That's the whole system. New plate, one snap, eat. Three plates = three photos = three entries. KiloKaki reads each photo, estimates the components together, and logs the plate as one entry. No stocktake, no dropdown menus.
  2. Log rounds, not items. Resist itemising the nine things on the plate. "Plate 2: laksa, satay, some chilli crab" is the right resolution. The garnish doesn't need its own line item. Ever.
  3. Dessert is a plate too. The dessert round is the most skipped log of the meal — and often the densest. Same rule: assemble, snap, enjoy.
  4. Count the glasses. Two glasses of wine or three cups of fruit juice deserve one quick voice note: "two glasses white wine, one orange juice." Four seconds.
  5. Missed a plate? Log it after, badly. If plate three vanished before the photo, voice-log the memory: "another plate — roast beef, mash, a bit of pasta." A rough entry beats a blank one, every time.

Total logging effort for a two-hour buffet: about thirty seconds. That's the entire tax for keeping your data intact through the most chaotic meal format ever invented.

Eat the buffet

A buffet is not a failure state. It's a birthday, a reunion, a Sunday with your family — a planned, deliberate, delicious outlier. Logged, it's just a big data point that your week absorbs the way weeks always absorb one big meal. Unlogged, it's the start of the guilt spiral: a blank that spreads backwards into shame and forwards into "I'll restart Monday."

Snap the plate. Eat the food. Let the log be a record, not a bouncer.

You don't log a buffet dish-by-dish. You log it plate-by-plate: one photo per round, drinks in one line, dessert included. Thirty seconds of logging for two hours of eating — and no blackout in your data.

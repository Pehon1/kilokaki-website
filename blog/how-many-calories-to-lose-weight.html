How Many Calories Should I Actually Eat to Lose Weight?

The Honest Number — and Why Every Calculator Gets It Wrong

KiloKaki · July 8, 2026

You Google "how many calories to lose weight" and every calculator tells you exactly how much to eat. 1,500. 1,800. 2,000. A precise number that looks authoritative. And then you eat that number for a month and nothing happens.

Here's why: the number is an estimate based on an average. Your actual number is different — and the only way to know what it is, is to measure what you're actually eating.

Why Every Calculator Gets It Wrong (By 20–30%)

TDEE calculators use a formula based on your weight, height, age, and activity level. The standard one — the Mifflin-St Jeor equation — is actually pretty good. For an average person.

But "average person" has a standard deviation of roughly 20–30%. That means if the formula says 2,000 calories, your actual maintenance could be anywhere from 1,600 to 2,400. Eat 2,000 and you're in a 400-calorie deficit if you're at the low end — or eating at maintenance (or above) if you're at the high end.

The Singapore-Specific Problem

TDEE formulas are built on Western eating patterns and body compositions. Singaporeans have different average body compositions, different metabolic baselines, and — critically — eat a diet that these formulas weren't calibrated for. A formula built on chicken breast and broccoli data isn't perfectly calibrated for mee siam and char kway teow.

The Math Is Simple. The Biology Is Messy.

The deficit math is real: 3,500 calories equals roughly 1lb of body fat. A 500-calorie daily deficit equals ~1lb per week. That's not pseudoscience. It's physics.

But here's what makes it messy:

  • Water weight fluctuates. A high-salt meal, carb loading, menstrual cycles — these can mask fat loss for weeks. The scale moves sideways while you're actually losing.
  • Non-linear loss. Fat loss isn't a straight line. It comes in waves — plateau, drop, plateau, drop. People quit in the plateau.
  • The log vs reality gap. Most people eating "1,800 calories" are actually eating 2,100–2,200 (studies on self-reported intake consistently show under-reporting).

How to Find Your Actual Number

Step 1: Establish a Baseline

For 7 days, photo log everything — every meal, every drink, every snack — without changing what you eat. Just photograph it.

Step 2: Estimate Your Average Daily Intake

At the end of the week, go through your photos. Use this rough framework:

  • Breakfast: 400–600 calories (toast, eggs, cereal, Milo)
  • Lunch (hawker): 500–800 calories (cai fan, noodles, rice)
  • Dinner (hawker): 600–900 calories (typically largest meal)
  • Drinks: 100–300 calories each (Milo, teh, bubble tea)
  • Snacks: 100–300 calories each

Add it up. That's your approximate daily average. This is the number that actually matters — not what the calculator said.

Step 3: Set Your Target From Your Actual Number

Whatever your estimated average is, subtract 400–500 calories for a sustainable deficit. If you're eating ~2,200 calories by your photo log estimate, your target is 1,700–1,800.

If your average is already at 1,500 — you're probably under-eating, which causes metabolic adaptation. Focus on food quality and consistency, not further reduction.

The Problem With Eating Back Your Exercise Calories

Most fitness trackers and apps encourage you to "eat back" the calories you burn from exercise. This is one of the most reliable ways to stall weight loss.

Treadmill calculators say you burned 400 calories. You actually burned ~200. You eat 400 extra calories. You've just wiped out your entire deficit — and gained a justification to eat more because "you exercised."

If you're photo logging consistently, you don't need to eat back exercise calories unless you're doing multiple hours of structured exercise per day. For most people, the daily calorie target should be treated as a fixed number, not a variable tied to movement.

The Week-One Check-In

After one week of eating at your estimated target (from photo logs):

  • Scale dropped 0.5–1kg: You're in the right direction. Keep going.
  • Scale flat: Your estimate is off — you're eating more than you think. Look at your photos again. Are drinks being logged? Oils? Sauces?
  • Scale went up: You're overeating. Cut portions or reduce the frequency of the highest-calorie items in your photos.

The photos tell the story. The scale confirms it.

The Only Number That Matters

Forget the calculator number. Your actual number is whatever you're eating right now that has your weight stable. Photo log a week of that, find the average, subtract 400–500 calories, and that's your real target.

Why You Plateau at Week 3 (And the One Tweak That Fixes It)

The scale stopped moving. You're still doing everything right. The problem? Your calorie target is based on the body you had three weeks ago — not the one you have now.

6-7 min read

You started strong. Week 1: down 0.8 kg. Week 2: down another 0.5 kg. You felt it — the momentum, the confidence, the "this is actually working" energy.

Then Week 3 hits. The scale doesn't move. Or it moves, then bounces right back. You're doing everything right — same deficit, same consistency, same effort — and nothing.

Welcome to the plateau. It's not your fault, and it's not permanent. But it does mean one thing: your body has changed, and your calorie target hasn't caught up.

The Math That Betrays You

Here's what almost nobody tells you about calorie deficits: they're not fixed.

Your TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) is the number of calories you burn each day just being alive — breathing, walking, digesting, thinking, existing. It's not a constant. It's a function of your body weight.

The heavier you are, the more energy it takes to move you around. The lighter you get, the less energy you need. Simple physics.

So when you started at 90 kg, your TDEE might have been ~2,700 calories. Eating 2,200 gave you a solid 500-calorie deficit. Solid progress.

But now you're 87 kg. Your TDEE has dropped to maybe ~2,600. You're still eating 2,200 — but your deficit has shrunk to 400. Then 300. Then, eventually, almost zero.

You haven't been slacking. You've been running on a shrinking deficit. The treadmill didn't speed up — the floor moved.

The Hidden Culprits

Weight loss doesn't just reduce the calories you burn through movement. It affects multiple components of your TDEE:

1. BMR drops. Basal Metabolic Rate — the calories you burn at rest — scales with body mass. Lose weight, and your body needs less fuel to keep the lights on. A 3 kg loss can reduce your BMR by 50–100 calories per day.

2. NEAT decreases. Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis — the fidgeting, pacing, standing, gesturing you do without thinking — unconsciously drops when you're in a calorie deficit. Your body is trying to conserve energy. Studies show NEAT can drop by 100–300 calories per day during weight loss. That's huge.

3. Exercise becomes cheaper. The same 30-minute jog that burned 300 calories at 90 kg might only burn 275 at 87 kg. Less mass to move = less energy required.

4. Adaptive thermogenesis. Your body is designed to survive scarcity. When you lose weight, it responds by becoming more efficient — squeezing more output from less input. This isn't "starvation mode" in the mythological sense. It's just biology. And it's real.

Combined, these effects can shrink your deficit by 200–400 calories per day without you changing a single thing about your diet or exercise routine. No wonder the scale stopped moving.

The plateau isn't just a physical phenomenon. It's the number one reason people quit. And the fix is stupidly simple — most people just never hear about it.

The One Tweak — Recalibrate Every 3–4 kg

Here's the fix: every time you lose 3–4 kg, recalculate your TDEE and adjust your calorie target.

It takes 30 seconds. And it restarts your progress.

How to do it

Step 1: Get your new TDEE. The simplest estimate: body weight in kg × 30 = maintenance. (This is a rough average — individual needs vary based on activity level, age, and body composition.)

Step 2: Subtract 300–500. That's your new daily target. If you were eating 2,200 at 90 kg, you might need to drop to 2,100 or 2,050 at 87 kg.

Step 3: Hold steady for another 3–4 kg of loss. Then repeat. Each recalibration keeps your deficit in the productive zone.

Why not just eat less from the start?

Two reasons:

1. Sustainability. Starting at 1,700 calories when you could start at 2,200 is just making yourself miserable for no reason. The bigger the deficit, the harder it is to stick with it. The harder it is to stick with it, the more likely you are to quit.

2. Muscle preservation. Crash deficits don't just burn fat — they burn muscle too. Preserving muscle matters for long-term metabolism, strength, and body composition. A moderate deficit (300–500 below TDEE) protects muscle better than an aggressive one.

The strategy isn't "eat as little as possible." It's "eat as much as you can while still losing weight." That's the deficit you can actually sustain.

When to Recalibrate — Signals to Watch For

You don't need to recalculate every day. Here are the signals that it's time:

The scale hasn't moved in 7–10 days. Not one or two days — that's normal fluctuation. But a full week or more of no change while you've been consistent? Time to adjust.

Your hunger has noticeably increased. Rising hunger in a deficit is a sign that your body is pushing back. A recalibration that restores a meaningful deficit can actually reduce hunger, because your body stops fighting as hard.

You've lost 3–4 kg since your last calculation. This is the proactive approach — adjust before the plateau hits, not after.

Your energy is dropping. Feeling more fatigued than usual during workouts or daily tasks? Your deficit might have eroded to the point where your body is conserving energy aggressively.

Why Logging Makes This Work

Here's the thing about recalibrating: it only works if you know where you started and where you are now.

You need your current weight (to recalculate TDEE), your recent average intake (to know if you're actually at your target or drifting), and the trend over time (to distinguish a real plateau from normal fluctuation).

All three require consistent logging. Not obsessive tracking — just knowing what you're eating and what the scale is doing.

This is why the Week 2–3 drop-off is so dangerous. The people who stop logging right when they need data the most are the ones who miss the recalibration window and quit.

The One-Tiny-Change Method

Stop overhauling everything. Pick one tiny friction point, fix it, let it compound. This is how people actually stick with food logging.

6 min read

The pattern shows up everywhere if you look. Someone starts measuring their cooking oil with a teaspoon instead of free-pouring from the bottle. A few hundred calories saved a day, no other changes, five pounds gone over a few weeks. Someone else switched to a smaller plate. Another stopped eating standing up. Tiny single changes, real results.

Not a single one of them overhauled their entire life on a Monday and never looked back. That's not how it works.

The problem with "change everything"

January is full of people who decide to eat clean, meal prep every Sunday, hit the gym four times a week, download a calorie tracker, start a meditation habit, and go to bed by 10pm. All at once.

By January 15th, most of them have quit. Not because they're lazy. Because changing five habits simultaneously is exhausting. Every new habit costs cognitive energy. Stack enough of them and your brain pushes back.

A 2010 study by Phillippa Lally at University College London found it takes 66 days on average for a new behaviour to become automatic. That's why piling on five new habits at once tends to break — your brain only has so much willpower to allocate, and most habits aren't yet running on autopilot.

The people who actually keep logging their food don't start with a perfect system. They start with one small change that makes logging slightly easier. Everything else follows from there.

The tiny change framework

Here's how it works:

1. Identify your biggest friction point. What's the thing that makes you skip logging? Not everything — just the one that comes up most often. For most people, it's the part where you have to search for the food, guess the portion, and manually enter it.

2. Replace it with a 10-second alternative. Not a 30-second alternative. Ten seconds or less. If the replacement takes longer than the original, you won't use it. The bar is low because it needs to be.

3. Repeat until it's automatic. Two to three weeks, typically. The point is repetition, not precision. A rough log done consistently beats a perfect log done twice.

4. Pick the next tiny change. Once the first one is automatic, you have room for another. Not before. Stack them slowly.

Apply this to food logging

If your biggest friction point is manual calorie entry — searching databases, guessing portions, adjusting macros — the tiny change is: take a photo instead.

That's it. One change. You're already pulling out your phone before you eat (let's be honest). Instead of opening a calorie app and searching, you snap a photo and send it. The AI handles the rest.

Don't try to also start voice logging, setting up meal reminders, analysing your weekly trends, and tracking your sodium. Do the photo thing for two weeks. Once it's automatic — once you do it without thinking — then add the next thing if you want to.

The goal isn't a perfect log from day one. The goal is a log that exists at all.

Why this actually works

Lowered activation energy. The hardest part of any habit is starting. A photo takes less effort than searching a database. Less effort means you're more likely to do it on day three, and day seven, and day fourteen — and that's when it stops feeling like effort.

Early wins build momentum. When you log five meals in a week instead of zero, you start to feel like someone who logs their food. That identity shift matters more than the data itself. People protect identities they're proud of — and abandon ones that feel like chores.

Each change compounds. Photo logging becomes automatic. Then voice logging removes the friction for meals you can't photograph. Then your daily reminder catches the meals you'd otherwise forget. Three tiny changes, stacked over six weeks, and you went from "I always forget to log" to "I log most meals without thinking."

Try it today

Here's the exercise. Pick one thing that makes food logging harder than it needs to be for you. Just one. Then find a way to do it in ten seconds or less.

For most people, that one thing is the gap between finishing a meal and logging it. The longer that gap, the less likely you are to log. The fix: log while the food is in front of you. A photo takes two seconds. That's the tiny change.

Come back in two weeks. If logging feels easier than it did before, the method worked. Then pick the next thing.

Food logging shouldn't require willpower. It should require a photo. If it requires more than that, the tool is the problem, not you.

KiloKaki vs MyFitnessPal

Which Food Logger Do People Actually Keep Using?

KiloKaki · April 17, 2026

MyFitnessPal has over 200 million registered users. KiloKaki has zero — because it doesn't track registered users, it tracks people who actually log their food.

Here's the uncomfortable truth about food logging apps: most people stop using them within three weeks. Third-party research on app retention suggests the median user drops off before the end of month one. The app with 200 million sign-ups is being used regularly by a small fraction of that number.

So the real comparison isn't "which app has more users." It's "which tool are people still using when the novelty wears off and life gets in the way?"

The Core Difference in One Sentence

MyFitnessPal asks you to build a habit around its system. KiloKaki fits into the habit you already have.

Everything else flows from that.

Where MyFitnessPal Wins

It's worth being honest here:

  • Nutritional precision. If you want exact macro counts, barcode scanning, and branded food lookups, MyFitnessPal's database is hard to beat. For bodybuilders, athletes, or people with strict macro targets, this matters.
  • Historical data. MyFitnessPal stores years of logs with detailed breakdowns. KiloKaki is newer and less established in this regard.
  • Ecosystem integrations. It connects to wearables, scales, and other health apps. KiloKaki doesn't.

Where KiloKaki Wins

1. Friction — The Actual Kill switch

The #1 reason people quit food logging is friction. The MyFitnessPal workflow is: open app → tap "Add Food" → search for "chicken rice" → scroll through 47 results → pick the closest match → adjust portion → save. That's if you're lucky enough to find it. If you can't find your actual meal, you're guesstimating anyway.

KiloKaki's workflow: open Telegram → send photo of your lunch tray → done.

The gap between "20 seconds" and "5 minutes" doesn't sound like much. But 5 minutes three times a day is 30 minutes of daily friction. That's the difference between a habit and a chore.

2. Honesty Over Accuracy Theater

Here's what MyFitnessPal's database model accidentally creates: people start selecting "lighter" versions of foods or under-reporting portions to match goals they've already set. The app rewards accuracy — but the accuracy is self-reported, and humans are biased.

A photo of your actual lunch tray doesn't let you lie to yourself. You can't select the "healthy option" version of your hawker meal. You can only show what's there.

KiloKaki trades MyFitnessPal's precision for honesty. For most people losing weight without medical supervision, honesty matters more than precision.

3. No Subscription Nudge

MyFitnessPal's free tier has become progressively more limited over the years. Key features — macro breakdowns, certain food databases, export capabilities — sit behind a paywall. The constant upsell is real and has been widely documented.

KiloKaki is free, no account required, no subscription. What you see is what you get.

The Real Question: Which One Gets Used?

Research on habit formation consistently shows that frequency builds habit, not perfection. Logging 80% of your meals with a quick photo beats logging 30% with a precise database — because the 80% user is still doing it six weeks later.

MyFitnessPal is a powerful tool for the right user: someone with specific macro targets, who doesn't mind the workflow, and who has the discipline to maintain it long-term. That's a real user. But it's not most people.

KiloKaki is built for everyone else — the person who's tried MyFitnessPal three times and quit three times, the person who eats hawker food that doesn't exist in any Western calorie database, the person who wants to track their food without it becoming a second job.

The Verdict

MyFitnessPal is better if you want nutritional precision and already have the discipline to use it. KiloKaki is better if you want to actually keep logging — because it removes the friction that's been stopping you.

Most people don't need a better food database. They need a food logger that doesn't feel like work.

The Sneaky Calories Hiding in Your Singapore Diet

Why 'Eating Clean' Isn't Working — And What Your Food Log Is Missing

KiloKaki · July 8, 2026

You eat cai fan. No fried rice. Vegetables and chicken breast. You turned down the char kway teow last Tuesday. You're being good — and yet the scale hasn't moved in three weeks.

Here's what most food logging misses: the calories that aren't in your main meal. The Milo with extra milk. The drizzle of kaya on your toast. The prawn crackers the auntie added without asking. The teh c peng that sounds harmless but comes with two spoonfuls of sugar.

These "invisible calories" are the reason many Singaporeans eat what seems like a clean diet and still can't lose weight. And the only way to see them is to photograph everything — including the drinks and the sides you think don't count.

The Milo Problem

A regular Teh-O is roughly 70 calories with one sugar. A regular Milo, with condensed milk, is closer to 250 calories. If you're drinking two mugs a day at work — which is completely normal in Singapore — that's 500 calories from Milo alone.

You're not logging the Milo because it's "just a drink." But two Milos a day is a meal's worth of calories that never makes it into your food diary.

Bubble Tea: The Weekend That Costs You a Workout

A standard bubble milk tea with less sugar is still 350–450 calories. Add pearl, add extra milk, get a large — you're at 600+ calories. One bubble tea on Saturday equals roughly an hour of jogging.

The problem isn't treating yourself. The problem is that it never gets logged because it doesn't feel like food. It's just a drink with your friends.

Photo logging doesn't stop you from having bubble tea. It just makes you see it for what it is — and decide consciously whether it's worth it.

The Hawker Oil Problem

Nasi lemak is not a health food. But you probably knew that. The more insidious problem is the "healthy" hawker meal that looks lean but comes swimming in oil.

  • Mee Siam — the broth looks light, but it's sweetened with sugar and belachan. One bowl can be 500–700 calories.
  • Wanton Mee dry — without the soup, the sauces, char siew, and fried wanton skins add up fast. 600–800 calories easily.
  • Fishball Noodles — the broth is salty and the fishballs are dense. A generous portion is 450–600 calories.

The portions look small. The plates are modest. The oil isn't visible until you photograph it and see the film on top of the broth.

The Kaya Toast Habit

Two slices of kaya toast with butter and two soft-boiled eggs sounds like a light breakfast. In reality:

  • Kaya (coconut jam) — ~120 calories per tablespoon, and the toast gets a generous spread
  • Butter — ~100 calories per tablespoon
  • Two soft-boiled eggs — ~150 calories
  • Two slices of toast with kaya and butter — ~350–400 calories

Total: ~620–700 calories for a breakfast that feels like "just toast."

The "One Spoon" Problem

Singaporean cooking involves sauces, condiments, and additions that add up before you notice:

  • Soy sauce on your rice: ~10–15 calories per drizzle
  • Chilli sauce on your noodles: ~20–30 calories
  • Ketchup on your fries: ~15 calories
  • Extra rice because the portion looked small: 150–200 calories

None of these get logged. They feel too small to matter. But across three meals a day, they're 150–300 calories of untracked energy.

Why Calorie Counting Apps Miss This

Apps like MyFitnessPal require you to search a database and log a specific portion. The problem is: how do you log the oil in your mee siam broth? The kaya on your toast? The prawn crackers the hawker automatically adds to your plate?

You can't. So people either skip logging these items entirely, or they guess wildly — and the guess is almost always wrong in the wrong direction.

Photo logging doesn't require a database. You photograph the actual plate of food — kaya spread, prawn crackers, oil sheen, extra rice — and you see exactly what's there. The photo becomes your log.

What You Actually See After a Week of Photo Logging

After seven days of photographing everything, most people see the same three patterns:

  1. The drink calories. Milo, teh, coffee with milk, juice — these are 200–500 calories that never entered their food diary.
  2. The oil and sauce multiplier. What looked like a lean dish turns out to be swimming in oil when you see the photo.
  3. The portion creep. The extra rice, the second helping, the prawn crackers added "automatically" by the hawker — these are 200–400 calories of unconscious eating.

The Fix Isn't a Better Diet. It's Seeing What You're Actually Eating.

You don't need to give up Milo. You don't need to stop eating cai fan. You just need to see what's in your food — consistently — so you can make decisions instead of just having habits.

Photo logging builds that awareness in 10 seconds a meal. No database required.

How to Start Seeing Your Invisible Calories

The rule is simple: photograph everything. Every Milo, every teh, every prawn cracker, every extra rice scoop. Don't judge it in the moment — just photograph it.

Once a week, look at your photos. Look for:

  • Drinks that repeat every day (and could be swapped or halved)
  • Oil or sauce that's consistent across meals (the oil is always there, not a one-off)
  • Portions that are larger than you thought (the photo doesn't lie)

One change — swapping your daily Milo for Teh-O, or cutting the prawn crackers — can create a 300–500 calorie daily deficit without changing anything else you're doing.

That's a week's worth of fat loss in one photo-induced decision.

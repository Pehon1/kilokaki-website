How to Log Cai Fan (Economy Rice): Singapore's Most Unpredictable Plate

Food Logging · July 2026

You point at the kailan. You point at the steamed egg. You hesitate — then point at the sweet and sour pork, because it's Wednesday and you deserve it. The aunty builds your plate in eight seconds, quotes you a price that follows no known formula, and sends you off with lunch.

Now try logging that.

Cai fan — economy rice, mixed rice, whatever your family calls it — is the single most eaten and least logged meal in Singapore. It has no menu. No standard portions. No two stalls, no two visits, no two scoops are the same. The dish you ate today literally does not exist in any food database, because it was assembled on the spot by an aunty with a ladle and opinions.

Which is exactly why it deserves its own guide.

Why cai fan breaks every tracking app

There's nothing to search for. Traditional calorie apps want you to find "your dish" in a database. But "cai fan" isn't a dish — it's a category with infinite combinations. Searching "economy rice 2 veg 1 meat" returns someone else's plate: their stall, their scoop, their sauce. The number you pick is a guess wearing a database's confidence.

The portions are whatever the ladle says. One stall's scoop of curry chicken is another stall's half-scoop. Ask for "less rice" and you might get 20% less or 50% less depending on who's serving. If your tracking method depends on knowing grams, cai fan defeats it before you've picked up your tray. That's the same wall we cover in "I don't know how much I ate" — except cai fan hits it three times per plate.

The sauce is invisible. Braised pork comes with braising liquid. Curry vegetables come swimming. The aunty's final flourish — a spoonful of curry over your rice — adds real calories that no database entry for "mixed vegetables" will ever capture.

So most people do one of two things: log "economy rice — 500 calories" and hope, or skip logging it entirely. Do that five lunches a week and your food log is fiction where it matters most.

What a cai fan plate actually costs

No label, no menu — but the components are knowable. Rough, honest ranges:

  • White rice, standard scoop: 200–250 kcal. The quiet baseline of every plate. Ask for less rice and you save around 70–100 kcal; add extra and it works the other way.
  • Leafy veg (kailan, chye sim, cabbage), stir-fried: 50–120 kcal per portion. The oil is the variable — hawker woks are generous.
  • Steamed egg, tofu, braised egg: 70–150 kcal. Honest, filling, mostly predictable.
  • Braised or steamed meat (soy sauce chicken, braised pork belly leaner cuts, steamed fish): 150–300 kcal. Pork belly sits at the top of that range. Fish at the bottom.
  • Anything battered and fried (sweet and sour pork, fried chicken cutlet, ngoh hiang): 250–400 kcal. The batter and the sauce are doing more work than the meat.
  • Curry sauce over rice: 100–200 kcal. The most invisible calories on the plate. Coconut-based curries especially.

Add it up: a restrained plate — rice, two veg, steamed protein — lands around 450–600 kcal. A Wednesday-you-deserve-it plate — rice with curry, sweet and sour pork, fried egg, one token vegetable — clears 800–1,000 kcal without looking any bigger on the tray.

Same stall. Same price range. Same-looking lunch. A 400-calorie difference, decided in eight seconds of pointing. That's why "economy rice — 500 calories" as a daily default quietly wrecks your data in both directions.

The fix: log the plate you actually got

Here's the good news: cai fan is the exact situation photo logging was built for. A database can't know your plate — but a photo of it can show every component at once. This is why photo logging beats manual entry in general, and cai fan is its strongest case in Singapore:

  1. Snap the plate before you eat. One photo, tray on the table. KiloKaki sees rice, identifies the dishes you pointed at, estimates each component, and logs the total. The eight-second plate gets an eight-second log.
  2. Name the sneaky stuff. If the curry went over your rice, or that's pork belly hiding under the kailan, add a line of context with the photo: "curry on rice, pork belly." The estimate gets sharper the moment the invisible stuff is named.
  3. No photo? Voice-log the components. Walking back to the office: "cai fan — rice, kailan, steamed egg, sweet and sour pork." Components, not grams. That sentence carries everything needed for a fair estimate.
  4. Round up on fried, round down on steamed. If you're unsure which end of a range your dish sits on, batter and sauce earn the higher guess. Steamed and braised earn the lower one. Then move on with your day.

Notice what's missing: no database search, no weighing, no picking "chinese mixed vegetables (1 cup)" from a dropdown and pretending it's your kailan. Log components, accept ranges, done.

Consistency beats precision — especially here

Your cai fan estimate will never be perfect. It doesn't need to be. If every plate is logged the same way — photo, components, honest ranges — your weekly picture is accurate even when any single lunch is ±100 kcal. Patterns emerge: the Wednesday fried-day habit, the curry-on-rice default, the way "one veg only" weeks feel different. That's the data doing its job.

The plate changes every day. Your method shouldn't.

Cai fan has no menu, no label, and no standard scoop — and it still takes eight seconds to log. Photograph the plate, name the sauce, round up on fried. The aunty doesn't measure. You don't have to either.

The App That Got Simpler While MyFitnessPal Got Complicated

2026 was the year food logging apps split in two.

Sometime in early 2026, a pattern emerged across r/loseit, r/nutrition, and half a dozen diet subreddits: people who had been happily logging on MyFitnessPal for years were suddenly, collectively, done.

Not because they'd reached their goals. Not because they'd stopped caring about calories. Because the app they'd used every day for three years suddenly started making every meal feel like paperwork.

Here's what happened — and why the simplest food-logging tool in 2026 isn't an app at all.

What Changed With MyFitnessPal in 2026

If you've been on MFP for a while, you know the timeline. The redesign that landed in April didn't just rearrange the UI — it added friction at every step:

More taps per meal. Logging a single dish went from "search, select, confirm" to a multi-screen flow with nutritional breakdowns, daily goal comparisons, and upgrade prompts baked into the logging flow itself.

Smaller calorie displays. The calorie count — the one number most dieters actually care about — got buried in expandable cards. You had to tap to see it.

Paywalled features. Barcode scanning, detailed macro breakdowns, and even basic meal planning moved behind the Premium wall. Features that had been free for years disappeared overnight.

Slower performance. The app got heavier. More ads, more tracking, more data to load before you could log a single meal.

Individually, each change was a minor annoyance. Together, they turned a 30-second habit into a 3-minute chore. And that's exactly why people started leaving.

The Friction That Makes People Quit

There's a pattern in food logging that every app company knows but very few act on: the harder logging is, the fewer days people do it.

Research on app retention is blunt about this. The median food-logging app user drops off within three weeks. The #1 reason isn't that they gave up on their diet — it's that the app became more work than it was worth. Every extra tap, every search that returned the wrong result, every moment of "wait, did I log breakfast?" is a chance to stop.

MyFitnessPal's 2026 redesign multiplied that friction across every step of the logging flow. And the users who felt it most acutely weren't newbies — they were the people who'd been logging for years and suddenly realised they were spending longer searching for "nasi lemak" than they had eating it.

"I just wanted to log my lunch. I didn't want to fill out a form" — as one Redditor put it, capturing the sentiment across dozens of threads after the redesign.

That sentiment — or something very close to it — appeared across dozens of Reddit threads in the months after the redesign. Not from people who didn't care about tracking. From people who cared too much to keep using an app that fought them on every meal.

What People Actually Want From a Food Logger

When you strip away the feature lists and the marketing pages, food logging comes down to three requirements:

  1. Fast. If it takes more than 10 seconds per meal, you won't do it consistently. Most people eat 3-5 times a day. That's 30 seconds to 2.5 minutes of overhead — every single day, for months.
  2. Accurate enough. You don't need 92 nutrients tracked to the milligram. You need to know if you're roughly in a calorie deficit. Good enough, every day, beats perfect, once a week.
  3. Low friction. No new app to download. No account to create. No database to search through. The fewer steps between "I just ate" and "it's logged," the more likely you are to actually log it.

Most apps optimise for feature depth. The ones that win on retention optimise for speed.
